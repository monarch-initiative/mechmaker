<h5>On this page:</h5><ul><li><a>Regulations and Responsibilities</a></li><li><a>Health</a></li><li><a>Bottled Water and Filters</a></li><li><a>Enforcement</a></li><li><a>Engagement</a></li><li><a>Lead Service Lines</a></li><li><a>Drinking Water Regulations and implementation</a></li><li><a>Air</a></li><li><a>Waste Water</a></li></ul><hr/><h2><a></a>Regulations and Responsibilities</h2><p><strong>How is the City of Flint improving its drinking water infrastructure and how is the City repairing any damage to private property during infrastructure improvements?</strong></p><ul><li>Check for <a>regularly updated information about Flint Water </a>and other Water Infrastructure News.</li><li>Check for information regarding the <a>status and funding of Flint's road/property restorations</a>. The number to call for road restoration inquiries is <a>810-410-1133</a>.</li><li>The <a>2021 Lead and Copper Rule Revisions</a> strengthens the requirements of the Lead and Copper Rule and mandates that only full (not partial) Lead Service Line Removals (LSLR) counts towards requirements. As of October 8th 2024, EPA finalized the  <a>Lead and Copper Rule Improvements</a> which  included changes to requirements related to Lead Service Line Replacements. <a>View the press release </a>about these changes. </li><li>View more <a>information on Lead Service Lines</a>. </li></ul><p><strong>How is federal funding being used in Flint and what oversight is provided to ensure it is used efficiently?</strong></p><ul><li>EPA manages Supplemental Drinking Water State Revolving Funds. In March of 2017, EPA awarded $100M of DWSRF to Michigan EGLE for water infrastructure in the City of Flint. EPA oversees each State in the way they administer and manage DWSRF.<ul><li><a>Drinking water State Revolving Funds Documents</a>.</li></ul></li><li><a>Michigan Water Infrastructure Funding and Financing Dashboard</a> is designed to showcase the current and historical projects funded through the <a>Clean Water State Revolving Fund</a> and <a>Drinking Water State Revolving Fund</a>.<ul><li>View <a>this data table </a>of this information.  </li></ul></li></ul><p><strong>How do I know if the water in my community meets SDWA compliance standards, including bacteria and chlorine levels?</strong></p><ul><li>Public right-to-know requirements are incorporated in the Safe Drinking Water Act, all community water systems must prepare and deliver an annual water quality report known as a <a>Consumer Confidence Report</a> for their customers in order to follow the Consumer Confidence Report Rule. View the City of Flint Consumer Confidence Report.  Additionally, <a>EPA's Public Notification Rule</a> requires public water systems to alert you if there is a problem with your drinking water.</li><li>Read a <a>detailed facility report</a> for the City of Flint.</li></ul><p><strong>Please explain how corrosion control works, including an explanation of how phosphate is used.</strong></p><ul><li>The<a> City of Flint states</a> “The City of Flint, the MDEQ, and the EPA have worked together closely to determine the necessary amount of Phosphate to be added to the water. Phosphate is a type of salt containing the natural mineral phosphorus and is added to water distribution systems across the country for corrosion control. The phosphates build-up a protective layer inside pipes so that metals like lead and copper do not leach into the water. This protective layer is known as scale and collects on the sides of the pipes by sticking to the metal. ” – Flint Increases Corrosion Control in Water</li><li>EPA’s <a>Optimal Corrosion Control Treatment Evaluation Technical Recommendations for Primacy Agencies and Public Water Systems document</a> provides an in-depth explanation of Corrosion Control Treatments established in the Lead and Copper Rule. </li></ul><p><strong>The Great Lakes Water Authority as recently increased the dose of orthophosphate to their water to better control corrosion of lead across their distribution system.  Will I see an increase of orthophosphate in my water in the City of Flint as a result?</strong></p><ul><li>No.  Great Lakes Water Authority’s increased orthophosphate doesn’t have any impact on Flint’s required minimum concentration of orthophosphate. Flint finished water quality shouldn’t change.  The City of Flint should be able to add less orthophosphate as GLWA adds more.</li></ul><p><strong>Please explain why my home is required to have a new water meter.</strong></p><ul><li>Flint residents have been mandated to replace their water meters to ensure that meters are working properly to determine household water bills based on accurate water usage. For up-to-date information on household water meters, visit the <a>City of Flint Progress Report webpage</a>.</li></ul><p><strong>How are water rates set and how can the city ensure affordability?</strong></p><ul><li>Cost of water is based on usage measured by home meters. See more <a>information about the home meter measurement</a> process.</li><li>The City of Flint just recently announced <a>a new water assistance program</a> through a program administered by the Genesee County Community Action Resource Department (GCCARD). </li></ul><p><strong>Who is responsible for testing and repair to plumbing in individual homes?</strong></p><ul><li>The water utility is responsible for the drinking water distribution system up to the service connection to the home or building. The homeowner or building owner is responsible for the service line and the premise plumbing system.</li></ul><p><strong>What are the primary and secondary sources of drinking water for the City of Flint?</strong></p><p>The primary supplier of drinking water is The Great Lakes Water Authority in Detroit and the secondary supplier of drinking water is Genesee County. Please refer to the <a>City of Flint FAQ website</a> for more information about this question. <br/><br/><strong>Are there any fish consumption advisories for fish caught in the Flint River?</strong></p><ul><li>See pages 24-28 for fish consumption guidance provided by Michigan Department of Health and Human Services in the <a>2023 Eat Safe Fish Guide</a> for Southeast Michigan.</li><li>How the Eat Safe Fish guidelines are made<ul><li>Learn about the<a> process MDHHS uses</a> to create the Eat Safe Fish guidelines.</li></ul></li><li>View<a> Reports &amp; Science page</a> for the  data used to create the Eat Safe Fish guidelines.</li></ul><h2><a></a>Health </h2><p><strong>What services are available to me, or my family, if we have been exposed to lead through drinking water or other media?</strong></p><ul><li>Lead exposure can affect nearly every system in the body. It may not have obvious symptoms, so people might not realize they have too much lead in their bodies. For young children, exposure to lead can cause behavior problems and learning disabilities. The only way to know if you have lead in your body is to get tested. Read <a>more information about getting tested</a>. </li><li>Children and young adults between the ages of 0 and 17 that have current open MDHHS Juvenile Justice cases may be eligible to receive a Comprehensive Trauma Assessment. Read more information on the<a> trauma assessment program and eligibility requirements</a>.<ul><li>View more information on <a>Juvenile Justice in Michigan</a>.</li></ul></li><li>Residents can have lead-based paint hazards remediated from their homes at NO COST through a special grant program facilitated by the City of Flint and funded through the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development. For a home to be considered for home lead abatement, the following requirements must be met:<ul><li>Home built before 1978.</li><li>Household includes children under the age of 6 or an expectant mother.</li><li>Property taxes are or will be paid. If uncertain, contact The City of Flint Lead-Based Paint Hazard Control Program Manager, Michael Carpenter, at 810-237-2019 or MCarpenter@cityofflint.com .</li><li>If the home is a rental property, the landlord must agree to rent the unit at affordable housing rates for 3 years after lead abatement is completed.</li><li>Household qualifies as moderate- to low-income (based on HUD guidelines, defined as earnings that are 80% or less than the area median income). </li><li>Residents can <a>request an application</a> (fill out a Google form) on the website.</li></ul></li><li>Visit the <a>Flint Registry</a> for additional services and programs to promote health and wellness.  </li><li>GHS offers a free 24/7 Crisis Hotline and Virtual Behavioral Health Urgent Care:  <a>810-257-3740</a>, as well as a 24/7 text line: Text FLINT to 741741 24 hours a day. <ul><li>Their website also has an <a>adult resource library</a> that consists of various sources of resources for community members.</li></ul></li></ul><p><strong>Where and how can I find testing for myself and my family if I have been exposed to lead from drinking water?</strong></p><ul><li>Adults currently in their 20s who were children during the water crisis are encouraged to speak with their doctor, primary health care provider, psychiatrist, or neurologist to discuss any available neurodevelopmental testing for adults. Any children between the age of 3 and 17 who have yet to graduate high school, have lived, worked, or attended day-care/school in Flint or surrounding areas supplied by the lead-contaminated Flint water anytime from April 2014 to September 2021, currently resides in Genesee County, and has active Medicaid is eligible for a FREE brief screening to determine medical necessity for a neuropsychological assessment through <a>the Neurodevelopmental Center of Excellence</a>. <ul><li>Additionally, the <a>Flint Registry Annual Report 2023</a> states "the Flint Registry is a project that connects people to services and programs to promote health and wellness and helps understand how the Flint Water Crisis has affected the Flint community." - . The Flint Registry helps address public health concerns reported by Flint residents and connect them to resources available to support those affected by the water crisis. See more information about <a>the Flint Registry</a>.</li></ul></li><li><a>Michigan Lead Safe</a> has additional information on blood lead testing.</li><li>Children aged 0-5 can receive blood lead testing through the <a>Genesee County Health Department’s Women, Infants, and Children Clinic</a>.<ul><li>View <a>Women, Infants, and Children Clinic</a> for more information.</li></ul></li></ul><p><strong>Should I be concerned about lead exposure for myself or my family from other water uses such as laundry, dishes, or watering my garden?</strong></p><ul><li>No. It is safe to wash dishes and clothes with plain tap water but dry them after. Wash dishes, bottles, and toys with unfiltered soapy water. Dry before use. Lead in water will not be absorbed by porcelain, metal, or glass. Clothes washed in plain tap water will not contain enough lead to cause harm. </li><li>Your skin does not absorb lead in water. If plain tap water has too much lead, bathing and showering is still safe for children and adults. It is safe even if the skin has minor cuts or scrapes. Never drink bathwater, and do not allow babies and children to drink bathwater. Rashes have many causes, but no medical link between rashes and unfiltered water has been found. If you have concerns, call your primary care doctor and call 2-1-1. </li><li>"Irrigating (watering) your garden with lead-contaminated water does not significantly increase soil lead levels. If you are concerned about watering with lead-contaminated water, you can purchase a lead filter that attaches to your garden hose or purchase a rain barrel to make a rainwater catchment system" <br/><strong>Note:</strong> Sourced from <a><em>Frequently Asked Questions About Lead and Garden Soil</em></a>.<ul><li>Find additional about the research conducted in <em>How Might Flint’s Water Contamination Affect Garden Soils</em> <a>Part 1</a> and <a>Part 2</a>. </li></ul></li></ul><p><strong>Please provide information on any Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry investigations in Flint</strong></p><ul><li>Michigan Department of Health and Human Services conducted <a>a data review of cancer incidences</a> in the City of Flint compared to Genesee County and the State of Michigan for the years 2000-2018. This study focused on specific cancers that could be associated with exposure to lead, per- and poly-fluoroalkyl substances, and/or total trihalomethanes; however, this study was designed only to be an overview and comparison of the cancer data available from the Cancer Registry Program. The cancer sites reviewed in this study associated with exposures included: colon, rectum, stomach, lung and bronchus, larynx, prostate gland, ovary, testes, urinary bladder, kidney and renal pelvis, brain and other central nervous system , and meningioma. This data review was released May 19, 2021.</li><li>Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry Flint Rash Investigation</li><li>Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry Flint Water Crisis, Michigan – 2016 <ul><li><a>Community Assessment for Public Health Emergency Response Fact Sheet</a>.</li><li><a>Community Assessment for Public Health Emergency Response After the Flint Water Crisis Report</a>.</li></ul></li></ul><p><strong>How can I learn about Legionnaires Disease in my community?</strong></p><ul><li>View the most recent <a>Genesse County Communicable Disease Report</a><ul><li>2024: Health Care Professional’s Guide to Disease Reporting in Michigan – Communicable disease reporting guidelines. </li></ul></li></ul><h2><a></a>Bottled Water and Filters</h2><p><strong>Who is responsible for providing bottled water in Flint?</strong></p><ul><li>Currently no parties are responsible for providing bottled water in Flint.</li><li>Bottled water provided by the State of Michigan was discontinued on April 7, 2018.</li><li>Bottled water provided by Blue Triton Brands was discontinued on December 31, 2022.<ul><li><em>City of Flint, EPA, Michigan EGLE</em> <a>advise Flint residents to continue using free water filters</a>.</li></ul></li></ul><p><strong>How were the filters determined to be effective and what chemicals are removed by a filter?</strong></p><ul><li>The <a>Flint, MI Filter Challenge Assessment</a> explains the process by which the filters were tested and the results. </li><li>Read through the external peer-reviewed research paper explaining  the efficacy evaluation of the point of use filters. </li><li><a>Look up your specific filter and what it reduces</a> or read through all <a>National Science Foundation filters </a>and what they reduce. </li></ul><p><strong>Where can I obtain a filter in my community and how do I learn to install and care for it?</strong></p><ul><li>Learn <a>filter pick up</a> information. </li><li>Learn where to <a>recycle water filters</a>. </li><li>Learn <a>how to install a filtration system</a>.</li></ul><p><strong>What is a better choice, bottled water or filtered water, and how do I make that choice for myself and my family? Should I use filtered water until my lead service line is removed?</strong></p><ul><li>To determine/evaluate the risk between filtered tap water or bottled water, it is important to know that drinking water standards apply to both sources of water. EPA regulates tap water, and the FDA regulates bottled water. If EPA makes any adjustments to the Safe Drinking Water Act, you should expect to see similar changes that reflect these in FDA regulations for bottled water.</li><li>Any individuals who feel they may be at risk from the consumption of filtered tap water, such as those who are immunocompromised or have weakened immune systems, should consult with a physician to develop an individualized safe drinking water plan to address raised health concerns. The Genesee County Community Health Access Program is one resource available that provides personalized screening and intervention to residents. <a>Learn more about this program</a>.<ul><li><a>Additional programs</a>.</li></ul></li><li>EPA’s press release, <a>Filtered Water Deemed Safe For Everyone In The Flint Community</a>,  states "Based on EPA’s findings and previous independent filter testing by Virginia Tech University researchers and National Science Foundation International which certifies water filters, all local, state and federal agencies involved in the Flint crisis response agree that the use of these National Science Foundation verified filters makes water safe from lead for all populations. Pregnant and nursing women and children no longer need to drink bottled water to avoid lead exposure as long as they drink water that has passed through a filter rated to remove lead from drinking water." </li></ul><h2><a></a>Enforcement</h2><p><br/><strong>Can the community be consulted / engaged in the enforcement process for SDWA or any other statute?</strong></p><ul><li>EPA is <a>restricted from communicating with parties external to itself about enforcement actions</a>. Continuing to implement these procedures will ensure an open and fair process and will allow enforcement staff to negotiate and conclude cases successfully. When sensitive enforcement information is released by EPA through either discussions or written communications, it may result in less protection of public health and the environment and jeopardize settlement negotiations. </li></ul><p><strong>Why does the enforcement process vary across communities and/or topics? </strong></p><ul><li>Enforcement orders vary in size and scope according to the assessed non-compliance. EPA cannot comment on the status of compliance with individual Orders and is prohibited from communicating with outside parties. Read more about <a>EPA’s Restrictions on Communicating with Outside Parties Regarding Enforcement Actions</a>. <ul><li><a>General Overview of the EPA Enforcement Process</a>.<ul><li><a>Water-Specific Enforcement Information</a>.</li></ul></li><li><a>Compliance Monitoring</a>.<ul><li><a>Safe Drinking Water Act-specific Compliance Monitoring</a>.</li></ul></li></ul></li></ul><h2><a></a>Engagement</h2><p><strong>How can Flint Residents engage on water issues?</strong></p><ul><li>Michigan Department of EGLE’s <a>Statewide Drinking Water Advisory Council</a>.</li><li>Flint City Council meetings are open for public attendance. See their <a>list of scheduled meetings</a>. Flint has formed a <a>Water System Advisory Council</a> that are also open to the public. </li><li><a>Water Systems Advisory Council Guidance</a>.<ul><li><a>Flint Water Systems Advisory Council Public Notices</a>.</li><li>EPA is providing logistical/organization support to the Water Systems Advisory Council through EPA Programs: <a>Technical Assistance Services for Communities</a> and <a>Environmental Collaboration and Conflict Resolution</a> support through the <a>Conflict Prevention and Resolution Services contract</a>. </li></ul></li></ul><p><strong>Who do I contact regarding drinking water in my home or community and how can I be informed of ongoing work that affects my drinking water? How do I elevate my concern if I do not receive a timely response?</strong></p><ul><li>The US Environmental Protection Agency enforces the <a>Public Notification Rule</a> as part of the Safe Drinking Water Act. This rule ensures notification to consumers if /when there is a problem or risk to public health posed from public drinking water.<ul><li>The Safe Drinking Water Act also “requires community water systems to prepare and provide to their customers annual consumer confidence reports on the quality of the water delivered by the systems. These reports will provide valuable information to customers of community water systems and allow them to make personal health-based decisions regarding their drinking water consumption.” </li><li>For detailed information see the <a>National Primary Drinking Water Regulations: Consumer Confidence Reports</a>.</li></ul></li><li><a>Stay up to date on the latest alerts</a> from the City of Flint concerning drinking water and sign up for the AlertFlint system and the CodeRED mobile app. <ul><li><a>City of Flint Office of Public Health Resources</a> website.</li><li><a>State of Michigan, Flint Resident Resources</a> website.</li></ul></li><li>If Flint residents are having problems with their water: <ul><li>They need to call the City Water Department and report it at <a>810-766-7202</a> (FAX: <a>810-742-6283</a>)<ul><li>Sewer Maintenance: <a>810-766-7079</a> (FAX: <a>810-742-6283</a>)</li></ul></li><li>If an issue goes unaddressed, <a>report it to EPA</a> the date that you first contacted the city and that the problem remains.</li></ul></li></ul><p><strong>How is clear and concise drinking water quality data and reporting made available to all residents, including non-English speakers and/or the hearing-impaired residents?</strong></p><ul><li><a>View </a>or <a>download</a> Water Supply Lead Results from Michigan Department of Health and Human Services. </li><li>The State of Michigan posts <a>various water sampling results</a>. </li><li>See the City of Flint Progress Report on Water and 2023 public water quality reports.</li><li>State of Michigan – Taking Action on Flint Website <ul><li><a>English</a>.</li><li><a>Arabic</a>.</li><li><a>American Sign Language  Videos</a>.</li><li><a>Mandarin – Chinese</a>.</li><li><a>Hmoob – Hmong</a>.</li><li><a>Espanol – Spanish</a>. </li></ul></li><li>EPA Flint Website <ul><li><a>English</a>.</li><li><a>Spanish</a>. </li></ul></li><li><a>City of Flint and Flint Latinx Partner on Spanish Translation Services </a></li></ul><h2><a></a>Lead Service Lines </h2><p><strong>Does removing the lead service line in my community reduce my exposure to lead?</strong></p><ul><li><a>Planning and Conducting Lead Service Line Replacement</a> website says “The most common sources of lead in drinking water are lead pipes, faucets, and fixtures. In homes with lead pipes that connect the home to the water main, also known as lead services lines, these pipes are typically the most significant source of lead in the water. Replacing lead service lines reduces lead exposure by removing this source.” </li><li>Residents still have potential exposures to lead from in home plumbing. The use of corrosion control reduces the risks and increases protection against exposure. Michigan has many services that may assist residents with the replacement of in-home plumbing to limit exposure to lead.</li><li><a>Learn more about these services</a>. </li></ul><p><strong>How can the city ensure that 100% of LSLs are identified and removed?</strong></p><ul><li>The City must follow EPA guidance on lead service line inventorying required under the <a>Lead and Copper Rule Revisions</a>. For more information see <a>Guidance for Developing and Maintaining a Lead Service Line Inventory</a>.<ul><li><a>Fact Sheet for Lead and Copper Rule Revisions</a>.</li></ul></li><li>The EPA has set a goal to replace 100% of lead service lines in the United States. For more information on this initiative, read the briefing on the <a>Lead Pipe and Paint Action Plan</a>. </li><li>Public Water Systems are required to monitor and report on lead and copper to follow the Safe Drinking Water Act.  Public Water Systems continue to monitor, report, and treat drinking water to reduce exposure to lead according to National Primary Drinking Water Regulations while lead service lines are being removed now and into the future.</li><li>See the geographic area of the <a>Community Water System Service Area</a>.</li><li>Michigan Department EGLE released a <a>brochure detailing information on the state of Michigan’s Lead and Copper Rule</a>.</li></ul><h2><a></a>Drinking Water Regulations and implementation </h2><p><strong>Who is responsible for LCR sampling in Flint?</strong></p><ul><li>The City of Flint is responsible for conducting testing at the required tier one and tier two sites and adhering to Michigan EGLE’s Lead and Copper Rule monitoring requirements and oversight. USEPA is responsible for providing oversight to State Agencies implementing the Safe Drinking Water Act.  Read more on the latest Lead and Copper Rule testing results on the <a>City of Flint</a> or Michigan Department EGLE websites. </li></ul><p><strong>What is the difference between National Primary Drinking Water Regulations and Secondary Drinking Water Standards? </strong></p><ul><li>National Primary Drinking Water Regulations are legally enforceable primary standards and treatment techniques that apply to public water systems. Primary standards and treatment techniques protect public health by limiting the levels of contaminants in drinking water.  Barium is a NPDWR.<ul><li>Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry <a>Public Health Statement for Barium</a>.</li><li>Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry <a>ToxFAQs about Barium</a>.</li><li>For an in-depth analyzation of Barium read the <a>Toxicological Profile for Barium and Barium Compounds</a>.</li></ul></li><li>EPA has established National Secondary Drinking Water Standards that set non-mandatory water quality standards for 15 contaminants. EPA does not enforce these "secondary maximum contaminant levels. They are established as guidelines to assist public water systems in managing their drinking water for aesthetic considerations, such as taste, color, and odor. These contaminants are not considered to present a risk to human health at the SMCL.<ul><li>Aluminum is a Secondary Drinking Water Standards <ul><li>Read more about the <a>National Secondary Drinking Water Standards</a>.</li><li>See the Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry <a>Public Health Statement for Aluminum</a>.</li><li>See  the Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry <a>ToxFAQs about Aluminum</a>.</li><li>For an in-depth analyzation of aluminum read the <a>Toxicological Profile for Aluminum</a>.</li></ul></li></ul></li></ul><p><strong>What is the role of the USEPA in Flint regarding regulation implementation?</strong></p><ul><li>The Safe Drinking Water Act requires EPA to <a>establish and enforce standards</a> that public drinking water systems must follow. EPA delegates <a>primary enforcement responsibility</a> (also called primacy) for public water systems to states and Indian Tribes if they meet certain requirements.<ul><li><a>Basic Information about Your Drinking Water</a></li></ul></li><li>EPA works closely with state partners to carry out our shared responsibilities under environmental laws. While Michigan has the authority to administer SDWA, EPA oversees Michigan’s implementation of SDWA and maintains its independent authorities to take action to help ensure public health protection.</li><li>As a state with primacy for implementing the <a>National Primary Drinking Water Regulations</a>, the Michigan Department EGLE is expected to fully implement all aspects of its safe drinking water statues and rules on which primacy is based. EPA conducts reviews for state programs regularly including the End-of-Year evaluation and reviews of state Public Water System Supervision Programs.<ul><li><a>Michigan Public Water System Supervision Program</a>.</li></ul></li><li><a>View an infographic</a> EPA created detailing how water systems work.</li><li>Read more about Michigan Department <a>EGLE’s drinking water program</a>.</li></ul><h2><a></a>Air </h2><p><strong>What is the current state of air quality in and around Flint, Michigan and how is that data collected?</strong></p><ul><li>See <a>current air quality data</a> from verified source. </li><li>See EPA Clean Air Act <a>compliance data for the City of Flint Cedar St. Reservoir Disinfection</a>. For general searchable EPA air related compliance data, visit the<a> EPA ECHO database</a>.  </li><li>See Michigan Department of EGLE’s <a>current air quality data and hourly reporting</a>. </li><li>For more information on EPA approved air monitoring methods, visit <a>Basic Information about Air Emissions Monitoring</a>.</li></ul><h2><a></a>Waste Water </h2><p><strong>How does the NPDES permitting process work and who is responsible for compliance in Flint?</strong></p><ul><li>The EPA has authorized the State of Michigan to administer the NPDES program in Michigan. This means that Michigan is responsible for deciding whether to issue or deny NPDES permit coverage to point source discharges of pollutants to waters of the United States that occur within the State of Michigan. While Michigan is the permitting authority, EPA retains the authority to oversee Michigan’s NPDES permitting program. The State and the EPA have the authority to carry out NPDES enforcement activities in Michigan. </li><li>The NPDES program is a diverse program covering many types of discharges to waters of the United States. For more information on the NPDES program, see <a>EPA’s NPDES website</a>. </li><li>For more information on Michigan EGLE’s NPDES program, see <a>EGLE’s NPDES website</a>.</li></ul>
